## The Blockchain Trilemma

Every cryptocurrency faces the **blockchain trilemma**: tension between decentralization, security, and scalability. Traditional linear chains face a tradeoff where pushing throughput often increases orphan/stale pressure or hardware demands.

Kaspa's BlockDAG architecture approaches this differently. Instead of trying to make one chain faster, Kaspa allows **many blocks to exist in parallel** and uses GHOSTDAG to order them. Throughput can increase via parallel block production while nodes still validate consensus rules locally.

<Callout>
The key insight behind Kaspa is that block ordering does not need to happen at block creation time. Miners produce blocks independently and concurrently, and GHOSTDAG later determines canonical ordering. This separation is central to how Kaspa addresses trilemma tradeoffs.
</Callout>

## Speed: Sub-Second Confirmations

Kaspa's current operating mode is **10 blocks per second** (about one block every 100 milliseconds). Applications can treat first inclusion as fast feedback, then apply additional DAA-score depth according to their own risk policy.

<Callout type="info">
Kaspa's speed comes from the BlockDAG + GHOSTDAG design and configured block rate. Confirmation policy is application-level and should be tuned to risk tolerance.
</Callout>

## Fairness: Reduced Orphan-Loss Effects

In traditional blockchains, when two miners find a block at nearly the same time, only one block survives. The other becomes an **orphan** — its miner receives no reward and the energy spent mining it is wasted. This creates a structural advantage for large mining operations with better network connectivity, since they can propagate blocks faster and win orphan races more often.

Kaspa's BlockDAG includes parallel valid blocks in consensus ordering. This has important implications for fairness:

- **Small miners tend toward hashrate-proportional rewards over long windows.** Reduced orphan races can lower variance penalties compared with strictly linear chains.
- **Latency asymmetry can be reduced.** Miners in higher-latency regions may see less penalty than in strictly single-chain orphan-race conditions.
- **Less wasted PoW from stale blocks.** Parallel block inclusion reduces traditional orphan-loss waste, while reward accounting still follows protocol rules.

## Scalability Through Parallel Block Production

Kaspa's throughput is not limited by the capacity of a single block. Instead, the aggregate transaction throughput is the sum of all parallel blocks produced across the network. At 10 BPS with a maximum block mass of 500,000, the network processes transactions across all blocks produced each second.

This parallelism also means scaling does not require only increasing individual block sizes (which can raise hardware requirements). Future throughput increases require protocol updates, parameter tuning, and hardfork coordination.

<Callout type="warn">
Higher block rates require careful tuning of consensus parameters. The GHOSTDAG `k` parameter must be recalculated to account for increased parallelism, and network propagation characteristics must be re-evaluated. This is why block rate increases go through a rigorous KIP process and hardfork activation rather than simple configuration changes.
</Callout>

## Proof-of-Work Sustainability

A common criticism of proof-of-work is energy consumption. Kaspa addresses this through two mechanisms:

**kHeavyHash mining algorithm.** Kaspa uses the kHeavyHash proof-of-work algorithm, which was specifically designed to be efficient on optical computing hardware. The algorithm's core operation — a 64x64 matrix multiplication — maps naturally to photonic processors. If optical mining hardware reaches production, it could reduce the energy cost of mining by orders of magnitude compared to traditional silicon ASICs.

**Reduced wasted work.** By including parallel blocks in DAG ordering, Kaspa reduces the stale-block loss that linear chains experience.

## Security Model

Kaspa inherits the full Nakamoto-style security model of proof-of-work:

- **51% attack threshold.** An attacker needs more than 50% of the network's hashrate to reliably perform double-spend attacks, the same threshold as Bitcoin.
- **No staking risks.** There are no slashing conditions, no "nothing at stake" problems, and no long-range attack vectors that affect proof-of-stake systems.
- **Objective consensus.** A new node can independently verify the entire history of the network from genesis without trusting any third party. There is no need for weak subjectivity checkpoints.
- **Permissionless participation.** Anyone can mine, run a node, or submit transactions without approval from any authority.

## Decentralization: Anyone Can Participate

Kaspa is designed so that full participation requires no permission and no stake:

- **Mining** requires compatible hardware and an internet connection. There is no protocol-level registration process.
- **Running a full node** requires modest hardware — an SSD, a modern CPU, and a standard broadband connection. Pruned nodes keep storage requirements bounded.
- **Protocol governance** follows the open KIP process. Anyone can propose a protocol change, and changes require broad community consensus to activate.

There are no validator sets, no staking minimums, no delegation mechanisms, and no governance tokens. Participation remains permissionless for miners and full-node operators.

<Callout>
Kaspa's proof-of-work design emphasizes permissionless participation and objective verification from protocol rules.
</Callout>
