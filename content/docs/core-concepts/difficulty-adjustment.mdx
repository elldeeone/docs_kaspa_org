## Overview

Kaspa's **Difficulty Adjustment Algorithm (DAA)** ensures that blocks are produced at a consistent target rate despite fluctuations in the network's total hash power. Unlike Bitcoin, which adjusts difficulty every 2,016 blocks (approximately two weeks), Kaspa adjusts difficulty **every single block**. This per-block adjustment allows the network to respond rapidly to changes in hash rate, maintaining stable block production even during large mining power swings.

<Callout>
Kaspa adjusts difficulty on every block, providing a much faster response to hash rate changes than Bitcoin's 2,016-block adjustment interval. This is essential at 10 BPS, where a slow adjustment could allow extended periods of incorrect block timing.
</Callout>

## Target Block Time

Kaspa's target block time is **100 milliseconds** (0.1 seconds), corresponding to a block rate of **10 blocks per second (10 BPS)**. The DAA continuously tunes the mining difficulty to maintain this rate as an average over its sampling window.

At 10 BPS, even modest delays in difficulty adjustment would be noticeable. If hash power doubled and the difficulty remained unchanged, the block rate would temporarily double to 20 BPS. Per-block adjustment minimizes the duration and magnitude of such deviations.

## DAA Window

The DAA operates over a fixed **window duration of 2,641 seconds** (~44 minutes). Within this window, **661 blocks** are sampled (one every 4 seconds) to compute the new difficulty. The algorithm examines the timestamps and difficulty targets of these sampled blocks.

The window parameters are carefully chosen:

- **Large enough** (2,641 seconds) to smooth out natural variance in block times caused by the probabilistic nature of mining.
- **Small enough** to respond to genuine hash rate changes in a timely manner.
- **Sample rate of 40 blocks** (4 seconds at 10 BPS) balances granularity against computational cost.
- **Sample count of 661** provides a broad window while keeping per-block computation practical.

## How the DAA Works

For each new block, the DAA computation proceeds as follows:

1. **Sample timestamps**: Collect timestamps from the sampled DAA window in the selected-parent context, including eligible mergeset blocks according to the window sampling rules.

2. **Compute the time span**: Calculate the elapsed time between the earliest and latest sampled timestamps. This represents the actual time taken to produce the blocks in the window.

3. **Compute the expected time**: Based on the target block rate (10 BPS), calculate how long the blocks in the window *should* have taken.

4. **Adjust the target**: If blocks were produced faster than expected, increase the difficulty (lower the target). If slower, decrease the difficulty (raise the target).

The adjustment formula ensures that the new difficulty is proportional to the ratio of expected time to actual time:

```
new_target = old_target × (actual_time / expected_time)
```

In practice, the sampled-window construction and expected-vs-observed duration ratio provide smoothing against short-term variance.

<Callout type="info">
The DAA is anchored on selected-parent context, but its sampled window also considers eligible mergeset blocks under the consensus sampling rules. It is not a simple "selected-parent-chain-only" timestamp average.
</Callout>

## DAA Score

Every block in Kaspa has a **DAA score** — a monotonically increasing consensus-time counter anchored on the selected parent and incremented by eligible mergeset contributions. The DAA score serves as a logical clock for the network:

```
block.daa_score = selected_parent.daa_score + (mergeset_size - mergeset_non_daa_count)
```

Where `mergeset_size` is the number of blocks in the new block's mergeset and `mergeset_non_daa_count` is the number of blocks whose blue score falls below the DAA window's lowest accepted blue score (these are excluded from the DAA score increment).

The DAA score is used throughout the protocol for:

- **Lock time**: Transaction lock times can be specified as DAA scores, providing block-production-rate-independent timing.
- **Block reward calculation**: The [emission schedule](/docs/core-concepts/emission-schedule) uses the DAA score to determine the current block reward.
- **Finality depth**: Finality and pruning thresholds are expressed using blue-score depth (not DAA score).
- **Difficulty window**: The DAA window boundaries are defined in terms of DAA score.

Because the DAA score increments by the mergeset size (which includes parallel blocks), it grows faster than a simple block count. At 10 BPS with typical parallelism, the DAA score increases at roughly 10 per second.

## Timestamp Manipulation Resistance

The DAA must be resistant to miners manipulating timestamps to artificially lower difficulty. Kaspa employs several defenses:

### Timestamp Bounds

Block timestamps must satisfy:
- **Lower bound**: A block's timestamp must be greater than the median timestamp of its past blocks. This prevents miners from using timestamps in the past.
- **Upper bound**: A block's timestamp must not be too far in the future relative to the receiving node's local clock. Blocks with future timestamps are temporarily rejected.

### Windowed Timestamp Span

The sampled DAA window uses a bounded timestamp span between sampled extremes (with additional window construction rules) rather than relying on a simple chain-median time-span formula. In practice, this reduces sensitivity to isolated timestamp outliers while keeping the adjustment responsive.

### Selected Chain Basis

By anchoring the calculation on selected-parent context and constrained sampled-window rules, the DAA limits the influence of arbitrary parallel-block timestamp manipulation.

## Minimum Difficulty

There is a **minimum difficulty** (equivalently, a maximum target value) that prevents the difficulty from dropping to zero. Even if all miners leave the network, the difficulty will not fall below this floor. This ensures that blocks always require meaningful proof-of-work.

## Comparison with Bitcoin's DAA

| Property | Kaspa DAA | Bitcoin DAA |
|---|---|---|
| Adjustment frequency | Every block | Every 2,016 blocks (~2 weeks) |
| Target block time | 100ms (10 BPS) | 10 minutes |
| Window size | 661 sampled blocks spanning 2,641 seconds (~44 min) | 2,016 blocks (~2 weeks) |
| Response to 2x hashrate | Minutes | ~2 weeks |
| Timestamp source | Sampled DAA window in selected-parent context (including eligible mergeset samples) | Linear chain |
| Timestamp protection | Median-based, bounded | Median-based, bounded |

The key advantage of Kaspa's approach is responsiveness. Bitcoin's two-week adjustment interval means that significant hash rate changes (such as miners switching between coins or large farms going online/offline) cause extended periods of abnormal block production. Kaspa's per-block adjustment keeps block times close to the target even during rapid hash rate changes.

## DAA at Different BPS Rates

The DAA is designed to be parametric with respect to the block rate. When Kaspa operated at 1 BPS, the same algorithm was used with adjusted parameters. The DAA window size and other constants are functions of the target BPS rate.

As block rates increase:
- The DAA window covers a shorter wall-clock duration for the same number of blocks.
- Adjustments become more granular, tracking hash rate changes more precisely.
- The DAA score grows proportionally faster.

This parametric design ensures the DAA remains effective as Kaspa's block rate evolves over time.

<Callout>
The DAA is one of the components that must be carefully recalibrated when the block rate changes. The Crescendo hardfork, which moved Kaspa from 1 BPS to 10 BPS, included updated DAA parameters to maintain proper difficulty adjustment at the higher rate.
</Callout>
